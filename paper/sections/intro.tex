\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Formal proofs often cross areas of mathematics. An inequality from analysis
may close with an algebraic identity, and a number theory goal may reduce to
a counting argument. A human prover notices these shifts and uses them: once
a goal looks combinatorial, the right lemmas and tactics change. Neural
provers for Lean \citep{yang2023leandojo,xin2025deepseekproverv15} have no
explicit notion of area. They sample tactics from a language model and expand
the most likely branches, so any knowledge of where a proof is heading lives
implicitly in the model's weights.

We first tried to find areas without labels, borrowing a tool from molecular
simulation. A Markov state model (MSM) describes a long trajectory through a
high-dimensional space as jumps between a modest number of discrete states,
and identifies sets of states the trajectory stays in for long stretches
before switching \citep{prinz2011markov,husic2018markov}. Here the trajectory
is the sequence of goal states in a proof, the clock is the proof step, and
the hypothesis was that the metastable sets correspond to areas of
mathematics. We fixed a test before collecting any labels: the metastable
sets had to agree with independent area labels at an NMI of at least 0.15
and beat a baseline that labels each proof by its Mathlib folder. They do
neither. One set absorbs most steps, the spectrum has no gap at any setting
we tried, and the result survives non-reversible and absorbing variants of
the model. Goal-state text changes far more between proofs than within one,
so geometry on goal states sees proofs, not areas.

We then measured areas directly. Mathematical areas carry no labels in Lean
or Mathlib, so two LLM judges label the goal before each step with a
top-level MSC 2020 code \citep{msc2020}, and we score the judges on benchmark
statements whose area the benchmark authors recorded. With these labels the
answer to the title question is: mostly not. A typical proof stays in one
area, and when it leaves, its off-area steps come in runs rather than single
steps. The lemmas a step cites tell a different story, mixing areas almost as
often as a random order would. Our reading is that Mathlib packs cross-area
moves into bridge lemmas, so the goal stays in one area while the library
does the crossing. A prover that wants cross-area knowledge will find it in
premise retrieval more than in the shape of the goal.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Step-level MSC 2020 labels for \resSwitchJudgeProofs{} traced Mathlib
  proofs from two LLM judges, with their agreement and their accuracy on a
  gold set built only from benchmark metadata
  \citep{zheng2022minif2f,tsoukalas2024putnambench}.
  \item A within-proof switch test against four nulls (a shuffle of each
  proof's own areas, a Markov chain per Mathlib folder, a folder pool and a
  judge-noise floor), which shows that proofs are area-coherent and that the
  steps they cite are not.
  \item A negative result with its gate fixed in advance: a Markov state model
  over goal-state embeddings \citep{yang2023leandojo} does not recover areas,
  across microstate counts, lags and reversible, non-reversible and absorbing
  fits.
  \item A paired test of an area prior in best-first search on miniF2F
  \citep{zheng2022minif2f} and PutnamBench
  \citep{tsoukalas2024putnambench}, which finds no change at a small budget.
\end{itemize}
